A matrix does two things to a vector: it turns it and stretches it. Feed in almost any direction and you get both. But most matrices have a few directions that come back pointing exactly where they started, just longer or shorter. Those are the eigenvectors, and the stretch factor is the eigenvalue.

Every matrix has a few directions it only stretches

\(A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\) is made up, not measured — one step of a two-group population model. These four whole numbers give eigenvalues of exactly 5 and 2, so the arithmetic stays out of the way. A real population matrix would have hundreds of dimensions and messy decimal eigenvalues, which you could not draw — and drawing it is the point. Also, \(A\) is not symmetric, which matters shortly.

numpy.linalg.eig hands you both at once:

Code 
import numpy as np

np.set_printoptions(precision=3, suppress=True)

A = np.array([[4.0, 1.0], [2.0, 3.0]])
vals, vecs = np.linalg.eig(A)

# eig always returns complex dtype for a non-symmetric matrix; both eigenvalues
# here are real. An eigenvector is defined only up to scale, so fix the sign too.
vals, vecs = vals.real, vecs.real
vecs = vecs * np.sign(vecs[0])

for lam, v in zip(vals, vecs.T):
    print(f"lambda = {lam:5.2f}   eigenvector = {v}   A @ v = {A @ v}")
lambda =  5.00   eigenvector = [0.707 0.707]   A @ v = [3.536 3.536]
lambda =  2.00   eigenvector = [ 0.447 -0.894]   A @ v = [ 0.894 -1.789]

Both do what they promise: A @ v is v with every number multiplied by the same amount — \((1, 1)\) by 5, \((1, -2)\) by 2.

\(A = PDP^{-1}\) is that idea written as a formula

Put the eigenvectors in the columns of \(P\), their eigenvalues down the diagonal of \(D\), and you can rebuild the matrix:

\[A = PDP^{-1}, \qquad P = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 & v_2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad D = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2 \end{bmatrix}.\]

Read it right to left: \(P^{-1}\) redescribes a vector in terms of the eigenvectors, \(D\) stretches each part by its own eigenvalue, and \(P\) converts it back. The eigenvalues come from solving \(\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\) — the characteristic equation, here \(\lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = 0\).

Multiplying the three back together gives \(A\), and drawing the map on a circle of unit vectors shows why the eigenvectors are special:

Code 
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

ACCENT = "#4A3AA7"
GREY = "#999999"

P = vecs
D = np.diag(vals)
print(f"max |A - P D P^-1| = {np.abs(A - P @ D @ np.linalg.inv(P)).max():.2e}")

theta = np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, 400)
circle = np.vstack([np.cos(theta), np.sin(theta)])
image = A @ circle

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.2, 6.2))
ax.axhline(0, color="0.85", lw=1, zorder=0)
ax.axvline(0, color="0.85", lw=1, zorder=0)
ax.plot(*circle, color=GREY, lw=1.3, label="unit circle")
ax.plot(*image, color="#444444", lw=1.6, label="image under $A$")

for k, (lam, v) in enumerate(zip(vals, vecs.T)):
    u = v / np.linalg.norm(v)
    ax.annotate(
        "",
        xy=lam * u,
        xytext=(0, 0),
        arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="-|>", color=ACCENT, lw=2.2, shrinkA=0, shrinkB=0),
        zorder=3,
    )
    # Mark where the eigenvector meets the unit circle: the arrow is that same
    # point scaled by lambda, and its tip lands back on the ellipse.
    ax.plot(*u, marker="o", ms=6, color="white", mec=ACCENT, mew=1.8, zorder=5)
    ax.plot([], [], color=ACCENT, lw=2.2, label=f"eigenvector, $\\lambda = {lam:.0f}$")
    ax.annotate(
        f"$\\lambda_{k + 1} = {lam:.0f}$",
        xy=lam * u,
        xytext=(16, 12 * np.sign(u[1])),
        textcoords="offset points",
        color=ACCENT,
        fontsize=11,
        va="center",
    )

ax.set_aspect("equal")
ax.set_xlim(-6, 6)
ax.set_ylim(-6, 6)
ax.grid(False)
for side in ("top", "right"):
    ax.spines[side].set_visible(False)
ax.legend(frameon=False, loc="upper left", fontsize=10)
ax.set_title("Dots: unit eigenvectors on the circle. Arrows: their images under $A$.",
             fontsize=10, color="0.3")
plt.show()
max |A - P D P^-1| = 8.88e-16

The circle becomes an ellipse, and the purple arrows come close to its long and short axes without landing on them. That near miss is real, not a bug: those axes belong to \(A\)’s singular vectors, which match the eigenvectors only for a symmetric matrix.

Diagonal matrices turn repeated work into simple arithmetic

The payoff comes when you apply \(A\) over and over. Each inner \(P^{-1}P\) cancels, leaving \(A^k = PD^kP^{-1}\) — raising a matrix to the hundredth power becomes raising two numbers to the hundredth power, and the bigger eigenvalue soon takes over. That is why it appears wherever the same map runs again and again.

Concept What it is Where it shows up
Eigenvector a direction \(v\) with \(Av = \lambda v\) PCA’s principal axes
Eigenvalue the scale factor \(\lambda\) along \(v\) variance explained
Characteristic equation \(\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\) finding the spectrum
Decomposition \(A = PDP^{-1}\) Markov chain steady states
When it exists \(n\) independent eigenvectors (diagonalizable) vibration normal modes

So the directions a matrix refuses to turn give you the viewpoint where it stops being a matrix and becomes a short list of numbers. It breaks for defective matrices, which lack enough independent eigenvectors to fill \(P\). And since this \(A\) is not symmetric, its eigenvectors are not at right angles, so \(P^{-1} \neq P^{\top}\) and the switch can go numerically wobbly — the reason Matrix Factorizations as Optimization Problems reaches for the SVD instead.
