Built for Problems It Has Never Seen

Why human intelligence is generalization, not skill — and why the LLM recipe rhymes with it

Skill at a known task can be bought with data. Sample-efficient generalization to a novel one cannot — which is what the human niche selected for, and what tools are the physical record of.

Hand someone a jammed mechanism they have never seen, a sign in a script they cannot read, a room whose social rules nobody explained. Most people get somewhere within minutes. Nobody drilled them on that particular latch, that alphabet or that room, and they were not retrieving a stored answer. They were working out which of the things they already knew might carry over.

That gap — between meeting a problem you have met before and coping with one you have not — is the subject here. It is also the thing our benchmarks are worst at measuring.

Skill can be bought; generalization has to be earned

The competence on display in that moment is not the interesting part. The exchange rate is: how little experience it took to get there. Skill at a known task is purchasable — enough repetitions, enough data, and a system plays the game well while learning nothing it could redeploy anywhere else. François Chollet’s On the Measure of Intelligence makes the distinction sharp. Score a system on how efficiently it converts experience into competence on problems it was never trained on — outside the training distribution, in the jargon — and memorization stops counting for anything.

Which raises a question, since that capacity is expensive: what was worth paying for it?

An unstable niche made the general-purpose engine worth its price

Humans never had a fixed task to specialize into: terrain, climate, food supply and social arrangement all shifted faster than genomes could track. An animal in that position has two options. It can compile behaviour into instinct — cheap to run, superbly tuned, catastrophically brittle the moment the environment moves. Or it can pay for a general inference engine that works things out inside a single lifetime: a metabolically ruinous brain, a childhood long enough to be a liability. Humans went hard on the second.

Tools are that engine’s visible output. Making one requires modelling a cause you cannot see, separating the means from the end, planning for a need that has not arrived yet, and carrying a technique to a material it was never developed on — the same operations a novel problem demands, in physical form.

But tools also drive the thing they express. Better calories helped fund the expensive tissue doing the thinking, and dexterity and planning circuits plausibly grew into each other. Then, once a technique could be taught rather than reinvented, each generation started from the last one’s best version instead of from nothing — a ratchet, which is what people mean by cumulative culture, and which put its own pressure on language and social cognition.

So the capacity is not one mechanism but three locked together: building a model of the world, passing what you learn to someone else, and practising against consequences. Machine learning has a paradigm for each of those three, and none of them alone gets you the whole thing.

No single paradigm reproduces it; the stack roughly does

Take the three in order. A toddler is nobody’s labelled dataset. They run raw experience through a predictor — what happens if I let go of this — and come out holding intuitive physics, object permanence and a causal map of the room. Learning from unlabelled data by predicting one part of it from another is self-supervised learning, and it is the closest machine analogue of childhood.

Then somebody who already knows shows you, corrects you mid-attempt, and hands you the word for it, so the technique arrives whole instead of being rediscovered. That high-fidelity channel is culture, and its counterpart in machine learning is supervised learning: every example paired with the right answer.

Neither of those covers the last part, which is that the tool works or it doesn’t. Acting, taking the consequence and adjusting on the strength of it is reinforcement learning — and at species scale it is evolution itself, with the reward paid in survival.

Any one of the three alone falls short: an unguided predictor, a mimic, or a specialist that burns a million trials on one task. Breadth comes from the integration — world model first, culture on top, practice sharpening the result. Which is roughly how a large language model is built, and not by coincidence: pretraining on a mountain of unlabelled text, then fine-tuning on curated examples of good answers, then reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), where people’s preferences between two responses supply the consequence. It is the best-known recipe aimed at the same target.

The rhyme stops short of identity. A child’s world model is built by intervening in the world, not by reading descriptions of it, and their culture arrives from people who correct them in real time rather than from a fixed pile of examples. Still, the framing survives, and it is what the jammed latch was about: the benchmark that matters was never the task you trained for. It’s the one you’d never seen.
