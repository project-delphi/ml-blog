Six Views of PCA

Maximum variance, maximum projected energy, minimum reconstruction error, best rank-k approximation, covariance eigenvectors, truncated SVD — one optimization problem, written six ways, with the bridge derived rather than asserted.

PCA is not six algorithms. One projector identity turns minimum reconstruction error into maximum projected energy, the trace turns energy into variance, a Lagrange multiplier turns variance into the eigenvector equation, and the SVD closes the loop onto the best rank-k approximation. Then the chain is run on MovieLens genre ratings and a neutrophil metabolome, where the scaling choice and the noise floor decide the answer.

Open three references on PCA and you get three definitions. One says PCA finds the directions of largest variance. One says it finds the subspace minimising squared distance to the data. One says it is the truncated SVD of the centred matrix. Each states its own as the definition, and none of them mentions the other two.

That reads like three algorithms. It is one, and the bridge between the readings is about ten lines of algebra about a projection matrix. This post derives those lines, checks every step numerically, and then runs the chain end to end on two real matrices — user ratings from MovieLens, and a neutrophil metabolome — where the shape of the matrix decides which of the six readings is the one you can compute.

Two companion posts carry background. The Directions a Matrix Refuses to Turn is eigenvectors and eigenvalues from scratch. The Matrix That Rotates, Stretches, and Rotates Again is the geometry of \(U\), \(\Sigma\), and \(V\).

1 Notation

All six views use one matrix and one basis.

  • \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) — the data. Each of the \(n\) rows is an observation, each of the \(d\) columns a feature.
  • \(X\) is centred: every column has mean zero. PCA without this step answers a different question, and Section 10.7 says which one.
  • \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}\) — the basis we are looking for, with \(W^\top W = I_k\). Its \(k\) columns are orthonormal directions in feature space.
  • \(Z = XW \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}\) — the projection, or scores. Row \(i\) holds the \(k\) coordinates of observation \(i\) in the new basis.
  • \(\hat X = XWW^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) — the reconstruction. Back in the original \(d\) features, but of rank at most \(k\).
  • \(P = WW^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\) — the orthogonal projector onto the span of \(W\)’s columns. \(P^2 = P\) and \(P^\top = P\).

Projection and reconstruction are different objects and get confused constantly. \(Z\) is \(n \times k\) and lives in the small space. \(\hat X\) is \(n \times d\) and lives back in the big one, on a \(k\)-dimensional flat inside it.

2 The six views

Each of these is a complete definition of PCA, and each one is what some textbook opens with.

  1. Maximum variance. Find the directions along which the projected observations spread out the most.
  2. Maximum projected energy. Find \(W\) maximising \(\|XW\|_F^2\), the total squared magnitude of the projected representation.
  3. Minimum reconstruction error. Find the \(k\)-dimensional subspace minimising \(\|X - XWW^\top\|_F^2\), the squared perpendicular distance from the data to the subspace.
  4. Best rank-\(k\) approximation. Find the rank-\(k\) matrix closest to \(X\) in Frobenius norm.
  5. Covariance eigendecomposition. Take the leading eigenvectors of the sample covariance matrix.
  6. Truncated SVD. Take the leading right singular vectors of \(X\).

They are not six independent problems. Views 1, 2, and 3 are one optimization problem written in three notations. Views 5 and 6 are two routes to the solution of that problem, not separate objectives. View 4 is the same optimum read as a statement about matrices rather than about subspaces. Section 10 does the work of showing that.

3 Maximum variance

Start with \(k = 1\). A unit direction \(w \in \mathbb{R}^d\) turns each observation \(x_i\) into the single number \(x_i^\top w\), and because \(X\) is centred those \(n\) numbers already have mean zero. Their sample variance is

\[ \operatorname{Var}(Xw) = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i^\top w)^2 = \frac{w^\top X^\top X w}{n-1}. \]

Maximising that over unit \(w\) is maximising the Rayleigh quotient of \(X^\top X\):

\[ w_1 = \arg\max_{w \neq 0} \frac{w^\top X^\top X w}{w^\top w}. \]

The quotient measures how much \(X^\top X\) stretches a direction, with the length of \(w\) divided out so that only the direction matters. Its maximum is the largest eigenvalue of \(X^\top X\), attained at the matching eigenvector — that is the Rayleigh–Ritz theorem, and Section 10.3 derives it from the constraint rather than quoting it.

For \(k\) components, sum the variances of the \(k\) projected coordinates. That sum is a trace:

\[ \max_{W^\top W = I_k} \ \operatorname{tr}\!\left(W^\top \frac{X^\top X}{n-1} W\right). \]

\(W^\top X^\top X W\) is \(k \times k\); its diagonal holds the \(k\) projected variances (times \(n-1\)) and the trace adds them up. The constraint \(W^\top W = I_k\) is what stops the answer being \(k\) copies of \(w_1\).

4 Maximum projected energy

Energy is the signal-processing name for a sum of squared magnitudes. A discrete signal \(s[1], \dots, s[T]\) has energy \(\sum_t |s[t]|^2\); a vector has energy \(\|v\|_2^2\); a matrix has energy

\[ \|Z\|_F^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k} Z_{ij}^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \|z_i\|_2^2 . \]

With \(Z = XW\) this is \(\|XW\|_F^2\): add up the squared length of every observation’s projected coordinate vector. It says how much of the data’s squared magnitude survives the projection.

The reason the word transfers from signals to matrices is Parseval’s identity. An orthonormal change of basis moves energy between coordinates but never creates or destroys it, so a full orthonormal \(W\) with \(k = d\) gives \(\|XW\|_F^2 = \|X\|_F^2\) exactly. Truncating to \(k < d\) throws some away, and PCA is the choice of which part to keep.

For centred \(X\) the connection to variance is arithmetic. Column \(j\) of \(Z\) has mean zero, because the column means of \(Z\) are the column means of \(X\) times \(W\), and those are zero. So \(\sum_i Z_{ij}^2 = (n-1)\operatorname{Var}(Z_{:,j})\), and

\[ \|XW\|_F^2 = (n-1) \sum_{j=1}^{k} \operatorname{Var}(Z_{:,j}). \]

Projected energy is total projected variance times the constant \(n-1\). Maximising one maximises the other, over the same \(W\), with the same maximiser. Energy is the matrix-norm writing of view 1, not a second algorithm. The only thing it adds is a norm that the next two views also speak.

5 Minimum reconstruction error

Project, then come back: \(Z = XW\) drops each observation to \(k\) numbers, and \(\hat X = ZW^\top = XWW^\top\) lifts those numbers back into the original \(d\) features. PCA in this reading is

\[ \min_{W^\top W = I_k} \ \|X - XWW^\top\|_F^2 . \]

Geometrically, \(\hat x_i = WW^\top x_i\) is the closest point to \(x_i\) on the subspace spanned by \(W\), and \(x_i - \hat x_i\) is perpendicular to that subspace. The objective is the total squared length of those perpendiculars. PCA is the \(k\)-dimensional flat through the origin — the origin because \(X\) is centred — that the cloud sits closest to.

This is the one view that is not obviously about variance at all. It asks for a good fit, not a large spread. Section 10.1 is the line that makes them the same question.

6 Best rank-\(k\) approximation

Forget subspaces and ask for a matrix. Among all \(n \times d\) matrices \(B\) of rank at most \(k\), which is closest to \(X\)?

\[ \min_{\operatorname{rank}(B) \le k} \|X - B\|_F^2 . \]

The Eckart–Young–Mirsky theorem answers it: the minimiser is the truncated SVD \(B^\star = U_k\Sigma_kV_k^\top\), and the minimum value is \(\sum_{i > k}\sigma_i^2\), the energy in the singular values you dropped.

The distinction the two framings hide:

  • Finding a subspace returns a \(k\)-dimensional flat. Any \(W\) whose columns span it is an equally valid answer, so \(W\) is determined only up to a \(k \times k\) orthogonal rotation. The genuine object is the subspace.
  • Approximating a matrix returns one matrix \(\hat X\). It is unique as soon as \(\sigma_k > \sigma_{k+1}\), and it does not change when you rotate \(W\) inside its own span, because \(WW^\top\) does not change.

So \(\hat X\) is the invariant; the loadings are a chart on it. That is why two PCA implementations can report visibly different loadings and identical reconstructions.

7 Covariance eigendecomposition

The sample covariance of centred \(X\) is

\[ S = \frac{X^\top X}{n-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}, \]

with \(n-1\), not \(n\), in the denominator. The \(n-1\) is Bessel’s correction: one degree of freedom went into estimating the mean we subtracted when we centred. The \(1/n\) convention is the maximum-likelihood estimate and is also in wide use — it scales every eigenvalue by \(n/(n-1)\) and moves no eigenvector at all, so it changes no principal direction and no explained-variance ratio. It changes reported eigenvalues, which is enough reason to say which one you used.

\(S\) is symmetric positive semi-definite, so it has an orthonormal eigenbasis with non-negative eigenvalues:

\[ S = V\Lambda V^\top, \qquad \Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d \ge 0). \]

The principal directions are the leading columns of \(V\), and \(\lambda_j\) is the variance of the data along direction \(v_j\) — set \(W = v_j\) in the trace objective and read it off. Because \(\operatorname{tr}(S) = \sum_j \lambda_j\) is the total variance across all \(d\) features, \(\lambda_j / \sum_i \lambda_i\) is the fraction of variance explained by component \(j\).

8 Covariance or correlation

Which matrix you hand to the six views is a decision, and it is made before any of them runs.

  • Covariance PCA works on \(S = X^\top X/(n-1)\) with the columns as measured.
  • Correlation PCA divides each column by its standard deviation first. The covariance of the standardised columns is the correlation matrix, so this is the same algorithm on a different matrix — not a seventh view.

The reason it matters is that \(\operatorname{tr}(S) = \sum_j \lambda_j\) is the sum of the column variances, so a column with a large variance brings a large share of the total for the components to compete over. Push that to the limit: if one column has a variance a thousand times any other, the direction of largest variance is that column, and PC1 reports the loudest instrument rather than the strongest pattern.

The sharper way to say it: covariance PCA is not invariant to rescaling a column. Measure a length in metres instead of centimetres and its variance falls by \(10^4\), the eigenvectors of \(S\) move, and PC1 changes — even though nothing about the data changed. So for features in different units, covariance PCA is answering a question that depends on a choice of units, which is rarely the question anyone meant. Correlation PCA removes the choice by making every column variance \(1\).

Neither is correct in general:

  • Correlation when columns carry different units or wildly different scales, or when you want every feature to get an equal vote. The cost is that a column which is pure noise is amplified to the same variance as a column carrying signal.
  • Covariance when the columns share a unit and their relative sizes are part of what you are measuring. Standardising then throws away real information.
  • Something else when a transform fits the measurement better than either. Dividing by an interquartile range rather than a standard deviation keeps a few extreme samples from setting the scale, and taking a log first fixes the skew a concentration usually has. Section 15 does both, for those reasons.

Both dataset sections settle this explicitly, and the two settle it differently: Section 14 has twelve columns on one \(1\)–\(5\) rating scale, and Section 15 has per-metabolite variances spanning ten orders of magnitude.

9 Singular value decomposition

The thin SVD of the centred matrix is

\[ X = U\Sigma V^\top, \qquad U \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}, \ \Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times r}, \ V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}, \]

with \(r = \min(n, d)\), \(U^\top U = V^\top V = I_r\), and \(\Sigma\) diagonal with \(\sigma_1 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r \ge 0\). Then

\[ X^\top X = V\Sigma U^\top U \Sigma V^\top = V\Sigma^2V^\top . \]

That is an eigendecomposition of \(X^\top X\) sitting in plain sight. Comparing it with \(S = V\Lambda V^\top\):

  • the covariance eigenvectors are the right singular vectors of \(X\);
  • \(\lambda_j = \sigma_j^2/(n-1)\);
  • the left singular vectors scaled by the singular values, \(U\Sigma\), are the scores \(Z\) for the full basis.

Eigenvectors and singular vectors are not the same kind of object — eigenvectors are defined for a square matrix by \(Av = \lambda v\), singular vectors come in left–right pairs for any shape via \(Av_j = \sigma_j u_j\) — and the identity \(X^\top X = V\Sigma^2V^\top\) is where they coincide for PCA: \(V\) is simultaneously the right singular vectors of \(X\) and the eigenvectors of the square matrix \(X^\top X\).

SVD is the computational route for two reasons. Forming \(X^\top X\) squares the condition number, so directions that were merely badly scaled in \(X\) become numerically indistinguishable in \(X^\top X\); and when \(d \gg n\) the covariance matrix is \(d \times d\) and singular, while the SVD never builds it. The Matrix That Rotates, Stretches, and Rotates Again makes the precision argument in full.

Views 5 and 6 are solution methods. They compute the answer to the optimization problem in views 1–3; they are not a seventh and eighth objective.

10 Equivalence

Six statements, one optimum. Four steps join them.

10.1 From reconstruction to energy

\(P = WW^\top\) is an orthogonal projector: \(P^\top = P\), and \(P^2 = WW^\top WW^\top = W(W^\top W)W^\top = WW^\top = P\) because \(W^\top W = I_k\). Expand the reconstruction error:

\[ \|X - XP\|_F^2 = \operatorname{tr}\!\left[(X - XP)^\top (X - XP)\right] = \operatorname{tr}(X^\top X) - 2\operatorname{tr}(X^\top X P) + \operatorname{tr}(P X^\top X P). \]

The last term collapses. Using the cyclic property of the trace and \(P^2 = P\),

\[ \operatorname{tr}(PX^\top XP) = \operatorname{tr}(X^\top X P P) = \operatorname{tr}(X^\top X P), \]

so two of the three terms cancel into one:

\[ \|X - XWW^\top\|_F^2 = \operatorname{tr}(X^\top X) - \operatorname{tr}(X^\top X WW^\top) = \|X\|_F^2 - \|XW\|_F^2 . \]

The last equality is the same cyclic move: \(\operatorname{tr}(X^\top X WW^\top) = \operatorname{tr}(W^\top X^\top X W) = \|XW\|_F^2\).

This is the Pythagorean split. Every observation’s squared length divides into the part the subspace keeps and the part it loses, with no cross term. And \(\|X\|_F^2\) does not contain \(W\):

\[ \min_{W^\top W = I} \|X - XWW^\top\|_F^2 \iff \max_{W^\top W = I} \|XW\|_F^2 . \]

Fitting the data as closely as possible and capturing as much energy as possible are the same instruction, because the two quantities sum to a constant.

10.2 From energy to variance

One line, already used in Section 10.1:

\[ \|XW\|_F^2 = \operatorname{tr}\!\left[(XW)^\top(XW)\right] = \operatorname{tr}(W^\top X^\top X W) = (n-1)\operatorname{tr}(W^\top S W). \]

\(W^\top X^\top X W\) is the \(k \times k\) covariance of the scores, up to the factor \(n-1\). Its trace is the total projected variance. So maximum energy is maximum projected variance, and the \((n-1)\) never affects the maximiser.

10.3 Where the eigenvectors come from

Nothing so far mentioned eigenvectors. They arrive as the solution of a constrained maximisation, and the constraint is what puts them there.

Maximising \(w^\top A w\) with \(A = X^\top X\) has no answer on its own: scale \(w\) up and the objective grows without bound. The constraint \(w^\top w = 1\) is what makes the problem well posed, and a constrained stationary point is found by Lagrange multipliers — add the constraint to the objective with an unknown coefficient, then look for a point where the combined function is flat in every direction:

\[ \mathcal{L}(w, \lambda) = w^\top A w - \lambda\,(w^\top w - 1). \]

Differentiating in \(w\) and setting the gradient to zero gives

\[ \nabla_w \mathcal{L} = 2Aw - 2\lambda w = 0 \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad Aw = \lambda w . \]

The eigenvector equation is the first-order condition. It was not assumed, and no eigendecomposition was invoked to get it: every stationary point of projected variance on the unit sphere is an eigenvector of \(X^\top X\), and nothing else is.

The multiplier is not bookkeeping either. At a stationary point,

\[ w^\top A w = w^\top (\lambda w) = \lambda\,w^\top w = \lambda , \]

so \(\lambda\) is the value of the objective there — the projected energy, and \(\lambda/(n-1)\) the projected variance. The \(d\) stationary points are the \(d\) eigenvectors, each scoring its own eigenvalue, so the maximum is the largest one and the argmax is its eigenvector. That is Rayleigh–Ritz, derived rather than quoted.

For \(k\) components the constraint \(W^\top W = I_k\) is \(k(k+1)/2\) scalar equations, so the multiplier is a symmetric \(k \times k\) matrix \(\Lambda\):

\[ \mathcal{L}(W, \Lambda) = \operatorname{tr}(W^\top A W) - \operatorname{tr}\!\left[\Lambda (W^\top W - I_k)\right], \qquad \nabla_W \mathcal{L} = 2AW - 2W\Lambda = 0 . \]

So \(AW = W\Lambda\). A stationary \(W\) need not hold eigenvectors — but \(\Lambda\) is symmetric, so \(\Lambda = RDR^\top\) for an orthogonal \(R\), and \(\widetilde W = WR\) satisfies \(A\widetilde W = \widetilde W D\). Its columns are eigenvectors, and it spans the same subspace as \(W\).

That is the rotation indeterminacy of Section 6 falling out of the derivation: the stationarity condition pins the subspace, and leaves the basis inside it free up to the orthogonal \(R\) that diagonalises the multiplier.

10.4 Which eigenvectors

Stationarity gives \(k\) eigenvectors; it does not say which \(k\). For that, take \(A\) symmetric with eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d\) and ask what maximises \(\operatorname{tr}(W^\top A W)\) over \(W^\top W = I_k\).

Write \(A = V\Lambda V^\top\) and set \(M = V^\top W\), which satisfies \(M^\top M = I_k\). Then

\[ \operatorname{tr}(W^\top A W) = \operatorname{tr}(M^\top \Lambda M) = \sum_{j=1}^{d} \lambda_j \, c_j, \qquad c_j = \sum_{l=1}^{k} M_{jl}^2 . \]

The weights \(c_j\) are constrained: each \(c_j \in [0, 1]\) because \(M\) has orthonormal columns, and \(\sum_j c_j = \|M\|_F^2 = k\). Maximising a weighted sum of the \(\lambda_j\) under “budget \(k\), at most \(1\) each” puts all of the budget on the \(k\) largest eigenvalues. The maximum is \(\lambda_1 + \dots + \lambda_k\), attained when \(W\) spans the leading \(k\) eigenvectors. That is the Ky Fan theorem, and at \(k = 1\) it is Rayleigh–Ritz.

Applied to \(A = X^\top X\): the maximiser is \(W = V_k\), the leading eigenvectors of \(X^\top X\), which are the leading eigenvectors of \(S\).

10.5 From eigenvectors to the SVD

\(X^\top X = V\Sigma^2V^\top\), so \(V_k\) is the first \(k\) right singular vectors of \(X\) and \(\lambda_j = \sigma_j^2/(n-1)\). The reconstruction at that optimum is

\[ \hat X = XV_kV_k^\top = U\Sigma V^\top V_kV_k^\top = U_k\Sigma_kV_k^\top , \]

which is the truncated SVD, which is Eckart–Young’s best rank-\(k\) approximation. The loop closes: the subspace that fits the data best reconstructs it as the matrix that approximates it best.

10.6 The chain

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} \min_{W}\ \|X - XWW^\top\|_F^2 &\iff \max_{W}\ \|XW\|_F^2 \iff \max_{W}\ \operatorname{tr}(W^\top X^\top X W) \\[2pt] &\iff W = V_k \ \text{(leading eigenvectors of } X^\top X\text{)} \\[2pt] &\iff \hat X = U_k\Sigma_kV_k^\top \ \text{(truncated SVD} = \text{best rank-}k\text{)} \end{aligned} } \]

Read left to right it is: fit, energy, variance, eigenvectors, singular vectors, low rank. Every arrow is an identity, not an approximation.

10.7 Assumptions

The chain holds under conditions, and each one is a real way to break it.

  • \(X\) is centred. Without it, \(X^\top X/(n-1)\) is a second-moment matrix, not a covariance, and its leading eigenvector chases the mean. The reconstruction view is then fitting a subspace through the origin rather than through the data.
  • Every entry is observed. The projector identity needs a complete \(X\). With missing cells there is no single \(\|X\|_F^2\) to hold fixed and the equivalence fails; Section 14 is the case.
  • The loss is Frobenius. Eckart–Young also holds in the spectral norm with the same minimiser, but an \(\ell_1\) or Huber loss gives a different optimum and none of these identities.
  • Feature scaling is fixed. Rescaling a column rescales its contribution to every trace, so covariance PCA changes answer when you change units. Section 8 sets out the choice; Section 15 measures what it costs to get wrong.
  • The spectrum has a gap at \(k\). If \(\sigma_k = \sigma_{k+1}\), the optimal subspace is not unique.
  • Signs and rotations are free. \(v_j\) and \(-v_j\) are both valid; within a tied block, so is any rotation. Reconstructions are unique where loadings are not.

11 Geometry of the equivalence

The synthetic cloud used here: 200 draws of a scalar latent \(t \sim N(0,1)\), mapped to \((2.2t,\ 0.9t)\) and perturbed by independent \(N(0, 0.45^2)\) noise in both coordinates, then column-centred. It is synthetic, its generating process is one line of NumPy, and it stands in for any measurement pair where one underlying quantity drives both readings and the instrument adds isotropic noise — two sensors on one process, two assays of one sample. Two dimensions only, because the point is to draw the residuals.

Code 
n = 200
latent = rng.normal(size=n)
X = np.column_stack([2.2 * latent, 0.9 * latent]) + rng.normal(scale=0.45, size=(n, 2))
X = X - X.mean(axis=0)

U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X, full_matrices=False)
pc1 = Vt[0]

thetas = np.linspace(0.0, np.pi, 3601)
dirs = np.vstack([np.cos(thetas), np.sin(thetas)])
proj = X @ dirs
energy = (proj**2).sum(axis=0)
resid = ((X[:, None, :] - proj[:, :, None] * dirs.T[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(axis=(0, 2))
total = float((X**2).sum())

fig, (ax_l, ax_r) = plt.subplots(
    1, 2, figsize=(9.2, 3.5), gridspec_kw={"width_ratios": [1.15, 1.0]}
)

scores = X @ pc1
feet = np.outer(scores, pc1)
ax_l.scatter(X[:, 0], X[:, 1], s=14, color=MUTED, alpha=0.45, zorder=2)
for i in range(0, n, 3):
    ax_l.plot(
        [X[i, 0], feet[i, 0]], [X[i, 1], feet[i, 1]],
        color=CORAL, lw=1.0, alpha=0.8, zorder=3,
    )
ax_l.scatter(feet[:, 0], feet[:, 1], s=9, color=ACCENT, zorder=4)
span = np.array([scores.min(), scores.max()])
ax_l.plot(span * pc1[0], span * pc1[1], color=ACCENT, lw=1.8, zorder=5)
ax_l.set_aspect("equal")
ax_l.set_xlabel("feature 1")
ax_l.set_ylabel("feature 2")
ax_l.set_title("Projection onto PC1, and what is left over")

deg = np.degrees(thetas)
ax_r.plot(deg, energy, color=ACCENT, lw=1.8)
ax_r.plot(deg, resid, color=CORAL, lw=1.8)
ax_r.plot(deg, energy + resid, color=TEAL, lw=1.4, ls="--")
ax_r.axvline(deg[energy.argmax()], color=MUTED, lw=0.9, ls=":")
ax_r.set_xlabel("direction angle (degrees)")
ax_r.set_ylabel("sum of squares")
ax_r.set_xlim(0, 180)
ax_r.set_ylim(0, total * 1.30)

# Label the curves on the curves: a legend box has nowhere to sit here.
ax_r.text(deg[energy.argmax()], total * 1.05, r"energy $\|Xw\|_F^2$",
          color=ACCENT, fontsize=8, ha="center")
ax_r.text(deg[resid.argmax()], total * 1.05, r"residual $\|X-Xww^\top\|_F^2$",
          color=CORAL, fontsize=8, ha="center")
ax_r.text(170, total * 0.92, r"sum $=\|X\|_F^2$",
          color=TEAL, fontsize=8, ha="right", va="bottom")
ax_r.set_title("One curve is the other, upside down")

fig.tight_layout(pad=1.1)
plt.show()

print(f"energy peaks at {deg[energy.argmax()]:.2f} deg")
print(f"residual bottoms at {deg[resid.argmin()]:.2f} deg")
print(f"sum varies by {np.ptp(energy + resid):.2e} across the sweep")
print(f"||X||_F^2 = {total:.4f}")

residual bottoms at 22.15 deg

The two curves peak and bottom out at the same angle, 22.15 degrees, and their sum is flat to \(2.84\times10^{-12}\) — floating-point noise on a total of 903.0973. That flat line is the identity of Section 10.1. Maximising the purple curve and minimising the orange one are the same search, because the dashed line does not move.

13 Numerical demonstration

The claims of Section 10 are checkable on the same synthetic cloud. The code is NumPy throughout, so the algebra stays visible; assert statements fail the render rather than the reader if a claim stops holding.

Code 
lam, vec = np.linalg.eigh(X.T @ X / (n - 1))
lam, vec = lam[::-1], vec[:, ::-1]          # eigh returns ascending order

print("singular values of X        ", S)
print("eigenvalues of S = XtX/(n-1)", lam)

14 Tech: MovieLens genre ratings

14.1 The data

  • Provenance. GroupLens ml-100k: 100,000 ratings from 943 users on 1,682 films, collected through the MovieLens site between September 1997 and April 1998 and released for research.
  • Collector and motive. The GroupLens lab at the University of Minnesota, building and evaluating recommender algorithms. Ratings are volunteered by people choosing what to rate, so they are not a random sample of opinions.
  • What this post uses. For each user, the mean rating they gave in each of the 12 most widely rated genres, keeping users with at least three rated films in every one of those genres. That is a complete \(313 \times 12\) table with no missing cells.
  • Why not the ratings matrix itself. 93.7% of the user-by-film grid is empty, and the equivalences need every entry observed (Section 10.7). Peeling the densest rows and columns until no cell is missing leaves a complete rectangle of 30 users by 28 films. That is a lower bound and not the maximum — finding the largest complete submatrix is the maximum edge biclique problem, which is NP-hard, so the honest claim is that greedy peeling finds nothing better. Aggregating to genres is the alternative, and it returns 313 complete rows instead of 30.
  • What one cell is. A mean over anywhere from 3 films to 410, and the table records only the mean. A companion file keeps the counts, because a cell averaging 3 opinions and a cell averaging 400 are not the same measurement and the difference decides what the spectrum can support.
  • Scale. All twelve columns are mean ratings on the same \(1\)–\(5\) scale, so Section 8’s units problem does not arise here — which is why this section can test the scaling choice rather than be forced into it.
  • What being wrong costs. Nothing clinical. A misread taste axis ships a worse recommendation.

14.2 The upstream ask

A recommender team wants to know how many taste dimensions the catalogue really has. Twelve genre columns is the measurement; the question is how many independent axes are hiding behind them, because that number sets how small a user profile can get before recommendations degrade.

That ask is well-posed for PCA and for nothing fancier. It asks for directions of largest spread and for how fast the spread runs out — which is views 1 and 3, and the answer is a spectrum.

14.4 Removing the rating scale

There are two ways to get the rating scale out of the answer, and it is worth being clear that they are nearly the same operation before choosing between them.

  • Drop it afterwards. Run PCA on the table as measured and throw PC1 away, keeping components 2 onwards.
  • Remove it first. Subtract each user’s own mean rating before centring the columns — the user-bias term every recommender baseline starts with — and then run PCA.
Code 
def principal_angles(A, B):
    """Angles in degrees between the column spaces of two matrices."""
    Qa, _ = np.linalg.qr(A)
    Qb, _ = np.linalg.qr(B)
    s = np.linalg.svd(Qa.T @ Qb, compute_uv=False)
    return np.degrees(np.arccos(np.clip(s, -1.0, 1.0)))

def bootstrap_wobble(M, k=1, n_boot=400, seed=11):
    """Largest principal angle between a resample's top-k subspace and the table's.

    Its own generator, not the shared `rng`: otherwise these figures move
    whenever an unrelated cell earlier in the post draws a different number of
    random values, and the prose quoting them goes stale silently.
    """
    boot = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    W0 = top_of(M, k)
    out = []
    for _ in range(n_boot):
        Wb = top_of(M[boot.integers(0, M.shape[0], M.shape[0])], k)
        out.append(principal_angles(W0, Wb).max())
    return np.array(out)

14.6 Downstream impact

The team asked how many taste dimensions the catalogue has. The honest answer is not the number they were expecting.

  • One axis is solid, and it is not taste. Rating generosity, 65.66% of the raw variance, stable to \(2.3^\circ\). It belongs in the model as a per-user offset, which is where recommender baselines have always put it, not in a taste profile.
  • A three-dimensional taste space, and one axis inside it worth naming. After debiasing and shrinking, the leading axis is Action/Sci-Fi/Adventure against Drama/Comedy, stable to \(10.2^\circ\). The three-dimensional span is stable too, at \(14.6^\circ\). What is not stable is the pair in between: the top-two subspace moves \(17.2^\circ\) with a tenth of resamples past \(57^\circ\), because components 2 and 3 are near-tied and swap. Ship three coordinates; name only the first.
  • Four components is over-reading. The tempting reading — four components, 71% of the debiased variance — counts noise as taste: 64% of that variance is sampling error in cell means, and in the thinnest columns almost all of it is. Mystery is 88% noise on a median of nine films per cell, and War 83% on eighteen.
  • The fix is more ratings per cell, not more components. Noise per cell falls as \(1/n_{ij}\), so the way to resolve a fourth axis is deeper sampling in the thin genres, not a larger \(k\) on this table.

The wider consequence is the assumption that forced the genre aggregation in the first place. On the raw user-by-film matrix, 93.7% of entries are missing, so \(\|X\|_F^2\) is not a fixed quantity and the projector identity of Section 10.1 has nothing to hold constant. Minimum reconstruction error over the observed entries and maximum variance stop being the same problem. That gap is why production recommenders fit a factorization to the observed cells directly — alternating least squares, or gradient descent on the observed residuals, both carrying the user-bias term this section arrived at the long way — instead of running PCA on a filled-in matrix.

15.3 The upstream ask

Before any metabolite-by-metabolite test, someone has to answer a quality-control question: is the biggest source of variation in this run the biology, or the machine? Run order, extraction batch, and column drift all produce large coherent shifts across hundreds of metabolites, and a per-metabolite test cannot tell them from signal.

PCA is the standard gate because it answers without being told the labels. Take the direction of largest variance, colour the scores by the label afterwards, and see whether the two agree. If the leading component tracks batch rather than severity, the tests downstream are measuring the machine.

15.4 Running the chain

The shape decides the method here. \(n = 75\) and \(d = 285\), so \(n \ll d\) and the covariance matrix is \(285 \times 285\) with rank at most \(n - 1 = 74\). It is singular by construction, and 211 of its eigenvalues are zero not because the data says so but because there are not enough samples for them to be anything else.

15.5 Downstream impact

The gate passes, and it passes weakly. That distinction is what running the gate buys.

  • What it licenses. The leading axis moves with severity rather than against it, so there is no evidence of a batch effect large enough to dominate the run. Differential-abundance testing can proceed.
  • What it refuses to license.   • What the rank bound costs. With 74 usable directions for 285 metabolites, rank-10 reconstruction still leaves 35.71% of the energy unexplained. 16 Probabilistic PCA

Views 1 through 6 are one optimization problem and two ways of solving it. Probabilistic PCA is a different kind of statement, and the difference matters more than the agreement.

It starts from a generative model rather than an objective. A latent \(z \in \mathbb{R}^k\) generates an observation:

\[ x = Wz + \mu + \varepsilon, \qquad z \sim N(0, I_k), \quad \varepsilon \sim N(0, \sigma^2 I_d), \]

so that marginally \(x \sim N(\mu, WW^\top + \sigma^2 I_d)\). Tipping and Bishop (1999) maximised that likelihood and found

\[ W_{\mathrm{ML}} = V_k(\Lambda_k - \sigma^2 I_k)^{1/2} R, \qquad \sigma^2_{\mathrm{ML}} = \frac{1}{d-k}\sum_{j=k+1}^{d}\lambda_j, \]

with \(V_k\) and \(\Lambda_k\) the leading eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the sample covariance, and \(R\) an arbitrary \(k \times k\) rotation. The columns of \(W_{\mathrm{ML}}\) span the principal subspace, the leftover noise variance is the average discarded eigenvalue, and as \(\sigma^2 \to 0\) the model’s posterior mean for \(z\) collapses onto the classical projection.

Where it is not the same statement:

  • \(W_{\mathrm{ML}}\) is not orthonormal. Its columns are scaled by \((\lambda_j - \sigma^2)^{1/2}\). The subspace matches; the basis does not, and \(R\) leaves it undetermined anyway.
  • It assumes a distribution. Views 1–6 assume nothing about how \(X\) arose. Probabilistic PCA assumes Gaussian latents and isotropic Gaussian noise, and those assumptions can be wrong in ways an algebraic identity cannot be.
  • It agrees at the optimum, not everywhere. The equivalences of Section 10 are identities that hold for every \(W\). This is a coincidence of maximisers between two different problems.

What the extra assumptions buy is the reason to pay for them: a likelihood, and therefore missing data handled by EM rather than by aggregation, mixtures of PCA models, Bayesian selection of \(k\), and a generative sampler. The MovieLens sparsity of Section 14 is a case where classical PCA has nothing to say and a probabilistic model does.

17 Constraints

Where the chain of Section 10.6 stops holding:

  • Missing entries. The projector identity needs every cell. ml-100k is 93.7% empty and the equivalence fails outright.
  • A non-Frobenius loss. Robust PCA and \(\ell_1\) variants optimise something else and reach a different subspace; Eckart–Young covers the Frobenius and spectral norms and no others.
  • Units and scaling. Covariance PCA on metabolite peak areas and correlation PCA on the same file are different questions, \(78.35^\circ\) apart. Section 8 has the choice; Section 15.2 has the bill.
  • Centring choice. Column centring and row centring give different leading components, as Section 14 measures — and which one is right is a statement about the measurement, not about the data.
  • Measurement noise sets a floor on \(k\). A component whose eigenvalue is the size of the sampling noise in the entries is not a direction. On the debiased MovieLens table, 64% of the variance is noise in the cell means and the leading direction moves \(20.3^\circ\) under resampling. The scree plot does not show this; only a noise model or a resample does.
  • \(n \ll d\). The rank of the covariance is capped at \(n-1\). Eigenvalues past that are zero by arithmetic, and any interpretation of them is interpretation of nothing.
  • Nonlinear structure. PCA fits a flat. A curved manifold gets a flat fitted to it, with no warning in the spectrum.
  • Signs and rotations. Loadings are determined only up to sign, and up to rotation inside a tied block — which is the multiplier matrix of Section 10.3 being free up to an orthogonal \(R\). Compare reconstructions, not loadings.

19 From matrices to tensors

PCA is the base case that makes higher-order factorizations legible, and the reason it is worth doing first is that the base case is the one where the six views coincide.

The chain of Section 10.6 runs on one fact that does not generalise: Eckart–Young. For matrices, the best rank-\(k\) approximation is the truncation of one decomposition, the optimal rank-\(k\) answer is nested inside the optimal rank-\((k{+}1)\) answer, and greedy is optimal. For tensors, all three fail. Truncated HOSVD is quasi-optimal, off by at most a factor \(\sqrt{N}\) in \(N\) modes but not optimal; the best rank-\(k\) CP decomposition need not exist at all, since the set of tensors of rank at most \(k\) is not closed; and CP components are not nested, so rank-2 is not rank-1 plus a correction.

So the tensor posts spend their effort on things PCA never has to decide: which product the operator lives under, which mode to unfold, which decomposition trades memory for accuracy on this cube. Tensor Factorizations and Tensor Inverses sets out CP, Tucker, TT, and t-SVD on a synthetic cube; Uses of Tensor Factorizations works the compression case. Both start from the assumption that a reader knows what a truncated SVD buys, and that is the assumption PCA supplies.
