What is a Biological Signature?

A fingerprint is a few features that stand in for a whole person. Finding one in a dataset is feature selection.

A fingerprint identifies a person from a patch of skin. A biological signature does it with molecules — and pulling one out of data takes four steps, of which the last is the one people get wrong.

A fingerprint identifies you from a patch of skin the size of a stamp. That is the trick: a few well-chosen features stand in for the whole person, and you never need the person.

A biological fingerprint does the same with molecules. A handful of genes, proteins, or metabolites — the small molecules a body makes as it burns fuel and builds tissue — carry a pattern, and the pattern is enough to tell one condition from another. This tumour and not that one. Infected, not healthy. When that pattern is measured rather than eyeballed, we call it a signature.

Finding one is feature selection

One lab test reads tens of thousands of genes at once; patients are slow and costly to enrol. A gene chip, or microarray, hands you 20,000 readings on 70 people, nearly all of them noise for your question, and a model given all 20,000 will fit that noise perfectly. The signature is the short list you keep.

  1. Measure wide. Every feature on every sample.
  2. Find the groups. If you already know who belongs to which group, you have what the next two steps need. If you don’t, let a clustering method propose the groups first.
  3. Select. Score each feature by how well it separates the groups. A t-test asks how far apart it puts the two group averages, relative to how much it wanders within each group. Mutual information asks a different question — how much does knowing this feature tell you about which group a sample is in — and so it can catch separations a difference of averages misses. Or let a model that is charged a price for every feature it uses (lasso, elastic net) drop the rest for you.
  4. Validate on data you never touched. Hold part of the data back, fit on the rest, test on the part you held back, and rotate — that is cross-validation, and each turn is a fold. Redo the selection inside every fold.

Skip step 4 and the number you report is too good. Choose the genes using all your samples and the ones you held back are no longer fresh: those genes were chosen because they split those exact samples. Ambroise and McLachlan (2002) showed this bias drags the reported cross-validation error towards zero, and that redoing selection inside each fold pushes it back up.

Golub’s fifty genes are the canonical case

Dodging that trap is what made the founding example credible. Two kinds of acute leukemia, ALL and AML, look alike down a microscope but take opposite chemotherapies, and the call rested on four separate specialist lab tests — good, yet errors happened. Golub and colleagues (1999) measured 6,817 genes in 38 samples from acute leukemia patients. Clustering, told nothing about who had which disease, nearly found the split on its own: it sorted the patients into two groups, one holding 24 ALL patients and a single AML, the other holding 10 AML patients and 3 ALL. They then trained a 50-gene predictor and tested it on 34 fresh samples from other leukemia banks. Fifty features out of 6,817: a signature.

The same four steps run on other data. Animals shed DNA into water, and a few marker sequences in a river sample say a jaguar was there. Read from starlight, an exoplanet’s atmosphere can show gases that chemistry alone would struggle to produce.

Further reading: Guyon and Elisseeff (2003) on feature selection; Meinshausen and Bühlmann (2010) on keeping only features that survive resampling.
